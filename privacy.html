Privacy

What StopScroll sees, does and keeps.

No internet, no ads, no account

StopScroll has no internet permission at all, so it cannot contact a server itself, ours or anyone else’s. It shows no ads and you do not sign in to anything. You can check the permission list in Android’s App info, or in the code.

The apps it watches

This version watches three apps: YouTube, Instagram and X. Facebook, Snapchat and LinkedIn appear in StopScroll’s list as coming soon. StopScroll does not watch them yet, and its code receives nothing from them.

When a new version adds an app, StopScroll does not watch that app until you have read the updated disclosure and agreed. Until then its code receives nothing from it. The apps you agreed to before keep working as they are.

What it looks at

StopScroll uses Android’s Accessibility Service, and only while you are in the YouTube, Instagram or X app. It reads the names and positions of the parts of the screen to see whether a short-video feed is open: the Shorts or Reels player, or X’s full-screen video viewer. It does not read the words on your screen: not your messages, captions, passwords or anything you type.

In Instagram, a Reel someone sends you in a message is closed too, like any other Reel. The chat itself, Stories, the home feed and profiles stay open.

In X, the feed is the full-screen video viewer, where you swipe up for the next video. X opens it when you tap a video in the timeline, a post, search or a profile, so StopScroll closes it there too, and a video you open full screen from a message closes too if X shows it in that viewer. Videos still play in the timeline and in posts, and a post with several photos or videos opens in X’s gallery, which stays open.

Each time it checks the screen, it first reads which app is in front. If that is not an app it watches, it stops there and reads nothing else.

What it presses

Back, twice if needed. If that does not leave the feed, one tab in the same app where there is one, then Home. Nothing else.

What it keeps

Your settings, the date you agreed to the disclosure, and for each app the number of feeds closed, the number of pauses after a loop and the date it last closed a feed. All of it stays on this phone; backups and phone-to-phone transfers leave it out. There is no history of what you watched and no screen content.

What Android does on its own

While an Accessibility service looks at a screen, Android’s own code inside the app can also receive notices of screen changes in other apps, for a cache Android keeps. StopScroll turns that cache off straight after each check, so this lasts only a moment and never happens while StopScroll is off. StopScroll’s code never sees these notices.

Android also reminds you, some time after you turn StopScroll on, that it can see your screen. That is expected.

The honest limit

On Android, an app update can add the internet permission without asking you. So the promise rests on the code, which is open source (MIT), and on the permission check every release build runs, not on Android asking again.

Where StopScroll comes from

StopScroll is published on Zapstore and GitHub. A separate edition for Google Play is built from the same source code, with the same lack of permissions. The store you use keeps its own record of the download; StopScroll itself sends nothing to anyone.
